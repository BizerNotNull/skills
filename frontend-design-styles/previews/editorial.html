Fiction

The Salt Cartographer

A surveyor maps a coastline that refuses to stay put.

Nadia Erram · 4,200 words

The instrument arrived in a wooden case the colour of weak tea, and for three weeks it sat on the windowsill doing nothing at all. My uncle had promised me a coastline. What he had actually promised, in the letter that reached me two days after his death, was the means to measure one.

Coastlines are not lines. This is the first thing a surveyor learns and the last thing he believes. A line implies a decision — here, and not there — and the sea makes no such decision. It lends you a shoreline for an afternoon, bills you for the wear, and reclaims the debt by morning.

I measured anyway. I set the tripod in wet sand, levelled it against a horizon that would not hold still, and recorded a number that was already a lie by the time the pencil left the page.

“Every map is a promise the sea has no intention of keeping.”

— from the fourth notebook
